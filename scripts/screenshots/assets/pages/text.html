How heat pumps keep a home warm

A heat pump does not burn anything to make heat. Instead it moves heat that already exists — from the outdoor air, the ground or a body of water — into your home, using the same refrigeration cycle that keeps a fridge cold.

Even on a cold day, outdoor air holds a lot of thermal energy. A refrigerant with a very low boiling point absorbs that energy and evaporates. A compressor then squeezes the vapour, which raises its temperature, and the hot refrigerant releases its heat indoors before the cycle starts again.

Because a heat pump moves heat rather than creating it, one unit of electricity can deliver three to four units of heat. Engineers call this the coefficient of performance, or COP. A modern air-source heat pump typically reaches a seasonal COP of around three.

Efficiency drops as the temperature difference between outside and inside grows, so heat pumps work best in well-insulated homes with large radiators or underfloor heating. Before buying, check your insulation and ask an installer to calculate the heat loss of every room.
